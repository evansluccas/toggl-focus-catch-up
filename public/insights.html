Toggl Focus · Product Manager assignment

The empty first week

A new freelancer can do everything Toggl Focus asks of them in week one — pick a use case, name a project, run a timer, open reports — and still be shown a blank chart, a dash where the client should be, and $0.00 earned. Not because they tracked too little. Because onboarding never asked the two questions that make their time mean anything.

Luccas Evans Prototype: source on GitHub Persona: freelancer

What I did

I signed up and watched week one fail

I created a fresh account and walked the entire first run as a freelancer would — choosing "See where time goes" at the fork, skipping the calendar connection the way most people will, naming one project, starting a timer, and following the product's own checklist to reports. Everything below is what I saw, not what I assumed.

01

Onboarding's only payoff is calendar import — and it's all-or-nothing. Step two leads with Google and Outlook, and auto-track defaults to on. Toggl has clearly identified "forgetting to track" as the core new-user problem. But it's the sole value-delivery mechanism in the flow, and it asks for an OAuth grant from someone who met the company ninety seconds ago. Skip it and onboarding delivers nothing at all.

02

It collects one field: a project name. No client, no rate, no billable flag. Afterwards the Projects table shows Client, Billable, Rate and Fixed fee all empty, and the project picker files it under a group literally labelled "No client" — the one dimension a freelance business is organised around.

03

The first calendar load opened on last week. A brand-new account's first impression of the core canvas was an empty grid for a week that had already passed.

04

Reports read "No logged time" while a timer was running. One minute twenty-three seconds on the clock, the entry plainly visible on the calendar, and every metric showed 0h. The checklist ticked "View your reports" as complete anyway — for visiting.

"Press play to start tracking — your timer starts running and the entry appears on your calendar."

Toggl Focus coach mark. The entry did not appear — the calendar was on last week.

The reframe that unlocked this: a freelancer who signs up on Wednesday has already worked Monday and Tuesday. Their week isn't empty — Toggl's record of it is. That gap is the product problem, and closing it needs no integration and no waiting.

Prioritisation

What I chose not to build

The W0 constraint is a hard filter, and it eliminates most of the pains in the brief. Knowing which client is profitable, estimating from historical data, defending scope against a contract — all need months of history. So I took the brief's challenge literally: rather than pick a smaller problem to fit the window, capture the contract shape early enough that the month-three questions can be answered in week one.

CandidateVerdictWhy
Reconstruct the week you already worked Built Value in minute one; no data dependency; serves the majority who skip OAuth
Ask for client, rate & budget during onboarding Rejected Fails when the freelancer doesn't have or won't share it — longer onboarding, same $0.00
Fix the first-session bugs Folded in Real, but you can't show the absence of an annoyance in sixty seconds
"Solo mode" — strip the team framing Rejected Right instinct, but it's ambient improvement, not a week-one moment
Prior art I found, and why it doesn't solve this

Toggl already has a Universal CSV Importer — buried in Admin settings → Data import, positioned for migration from Harvest, Clockify and ClickUp. So the capability to bulk-create projects and entries exists. But it's hidden where a week-one freelancer will never look, it demands a file and per-column mapping, it speaks in IT-migration language ("map data", "match existing records", with Members as the first entity type offered to a solo user), and Clients aren't an importable entity at all. Catch Up isn't a new capability — it's a freelancer-shaped front door on one Toggl already built and hid.

The solution

Catch Up

One flow, several on-ramps, one payoff. The calendar's empty state stops being a void and becomes an invitation: your week isn't empty, Toggl's just new here. Then a single box accepts whatever the freelancer has — spreadsheet rows pasted from Excel, notes-app text, or typing from memory. No "do you have a file?" question, because that question excludes people.

The one decision that carries it

The rate is asked for at the moment it pays off, not up front. When you say "three hours, Acme," that's the instant to ask what you bill Acme — because the answer immediately becomes money on screen rather than a field you fill in for a reason you'll discover later. The billable total updates live as you type, so the user causes the reveal instead of being shown it: $0.00 → $977.50 → $1,652.50.

And it degrades instead of collapsing. Skip the rates entirely and you still get your hours organised by client — which is more than the product gives you today.

Tolerant of format, conservative about meaning

The parser handles mixed durations (3h, 1.5, 09:00-12:30, 45m), inconsistent casing (acme and Acme become one client), and tab characters from a spreadsheet. But when it can't confidently read a line, it says so and marks it for you rather than inventing structure. Two bugs I found while testing pushed in exactly that direction: a prose note was turning its first word into a phantom client, and single-spaced input was splitting "Beta Corp" into "Beta". Both are fixed, and the principle they taught is in the code.

Tradeoffs

What I left on the table

  • CSV upload with column mapping. Linked, not rebuilt — requiring a file recreates the same dependency failure that killed the onboarding-fields approach.
  • Invoicing. The obvious next step once money is on screen, and firmly a week-two problem.
  • Profitability trends. Cannot exist in week one by definition.
  • AI inference of your day from browser history or documents. Deliberate: the user tells us, we don't guess. That's what keeps this honest and out of the much-travelled "let AI categorise your day" territory.
  • Team surfaces — approvals, capacity, member breakdowns. Wrong persona.

Impact

What I'd expect, and how I'd know

I'd ship this as an A/B test on the calendar empty state and read four things. The commercial argument matters as much as the activation one: a freelancer with a live contract is the better signup — they already have revenue and a reason to pay — and they're precisely the person who arrives with a week's work already behind them.

Primary Share of new signups with a non-zero Amount in their first week. Today this is near-zero by construction.
Retention D7 return rate, split by whether the user completed Catch Up.
Habit Reaching three or more entries across two or more separate days in week one.
Commercial Trial-to-paid conversion for the switcher segment specifically.

Guardrails I'd watch

Onboarding completion must not move, since we added no steps to it. And I'd watch the share of Catch Up entries later edited or deleted — if that's high, the parser is guessing too confidently and quietly costing people trust, which is worse than an empty week.

Assumptions

What I'm taking on faith

  • Most new signups skip the calendar connection. I'd validate this on day one; it's the single number this whole argument rests on. My reasoning is that it's an OAuth grant into a relationship ninety seconds old.
  • Most freelancers arrive with a live contract and some record of their time — a spreadsheet, notes, paper, or another tracker. Anyone billing a client must be keeping something, or they couldn't invoice.
  • Having a record isn't the same as handing it over. Some will decline to upload anything on day one, for the same trust reason they skip OAuth. Hence the typed path.
  • The CSV importer sees almost no week-one use, given where it lives.

Method

How I used AI, and where I overruled it

I used Claude throughout — to drive the browser through onboarding and capture the audit, to extract Toggl's real design tokens and icons so the prototype sits inside their system rather than approximating it, and to build. The product judgment stayed mine, and the two moments that mattered most were both disagreements.

It recommended the wrong direction first. Claude argued for capturing client, rate and budget during onboarding. I pushed back: if the freelancer doesn't have that information yet, or won't share it, we've made onboarding longer and delivered the same empty result — a net loss on the exact metric we're optimising. That objection killed the recommendation, and the good idea inside it (rate becomes money) got relocated to where it actually pays off.

It talked me out of the eventual winner, on a claim it couldn't support. Claude asserted that day reconstruction was the direction Toggl had flagged as overdone. When I asked for the evidence, it didn't hold — what was flagged was reviewing and categorising time that's already logged, which is a different problem from creating a record that was never captured. It also argued "Toggl already bet on calendar import here", which contradicted its own finding that import fails closed for everyone who skips it. Both corrections reopened the direction I built.

The lesson I'd carry into the role: AI is exceptional at breadth and speed — it audited a product and rebuilt its design system in an afternoon — and it will state a weak inference with the same confidence as a verified fact. The judgment worth paying for is knowing which is which.
